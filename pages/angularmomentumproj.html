James D Pickering

Orientations of Quantised Angular Momentum

This method is a little more hand-wavy than some and doesn't really lead to a robust answer to the question for spin angular momentum specifically - but it is reasonable enough to get a feel for the ideas.

We want to understand why there are \(2L+1\) different orientations (projections) of the quantised angular momentum \(L\).

Consider a particle moving on the surface of a sphere. The quantum mechanics of this system are well documented in any textbook (e.g Atkins), and the solutions are the spherical harmonics. The energy of the particle is given by:

$$ E = \frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2I} $$

Where \(l\) is the angular momentum quantum number and \(I\) is the moment of inertia of the particle. Classically, if we consider the same system, the energy of the particle is given by:

$$ E = \frac{L^2}{2I} $$

Where \(L\) is the angular momentum of the particle. Comparison of the two equations leads to the identification of the angular momentum, \(L\), as:

$$ L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\hbar ~( = \vert \vec{L} \vert) $$

Physically, we can interpret this as the length of the angular momentum vector, \(\vert\vec{L}\vert\), of the particle. This angular momentum vector could represent any angular momentum, though we have explicitly compared to the classical angular momentum \(L\) (so we won't see the half-integer angular momentum fall beautifully out of the equations).

We can represent this angular momentum in Cartesian coordinates, with components along each coordinate axis:

$$ \vec{L} = L_x \,\vec{i} + L_y\,\vec{j} + L_z\,\vec{k} $$

If you consider the observation of the angular momentum of a particle, you find that you can simultaneously observe only the square of the total angular momentum \(L^2\) and one of the components. By convention, this component is the z-component, \(L_z\). By analogy with the simpler system, the particle on a ring, you can identify \(L_z\) as:

$$ L_z = m \hbar, ~\text{where}~ m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, ... $$

This looks like we are getting somewhere familiar: we know that we expect \(m = -1, 0, 1\) for \(l=1\) (e.g. the three p-orbitals). But we have not seen a reason to limit the value of \(m\), yet.

Consider now a vector \(\vec{L}\) and it's z-component, \(L_z\). Clearly, the z-component cannot be longer than the original vector. So we have:

$$ L_z \leq \vert \vec{L} \vert $$

We know from earlier that we have expressions for both \(\vert \vec{L} \vert\) and \(L_z\), so:

$$ m\hbar \leq \sqrt{l(l+1)}\hbar $$

Dividing by \(\hbar\) and squaring:

$$ m^2 \leq l(l+1) $$

We know that \(m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, ...\) and that \(l = 0, 1, 2, ...\)

The inequality above is what brings the restriction on \(m\). For example, in the case that \(l=1\):

$$ \begin{align} l(l+1) &= 2 \\ m^2 &\leq 2 \\ m &\leq \pm \sqrt{2} ~(\pm 1.414) \end{align} $$

Thus we can see that the only values of \(m\) that satisfy \(m^2 \leq l(l+1)\) for \(l=1\) are \(m = -1, 0\) and 1, i.e three values of \(m\).

This is the basis of the rule that there are \(2l+1\) different values of \(m\) for a given integer \(l\).

We can also go a bit further and show that for a given integer value of \(l\), the largest integer \(m\) that satisfies \(m^2 \leq l(l+1)\) is always \(\vert m \vert = l\). One way to do this is a satisfying proof by contradiction. Suppose that the largest integer \(m\) that satisifes \(m^2 \leq l(l+1)\) is actually \(\vert m \vert = l+1\) (i.e. the next integer along to what we expect). Then we have:

$$ \begin{align} (l+1)^2 &\leq l(l+1) \\ l^2 + 2l + 1 &\leq l^2 + l \\ l + 1 &\leq 1 \end{align} $$

The last line is only true if \(l = 0\), and not in general. So \(\vert m \vert = l+1\) was obviously too big.

Now we try taking the next integer down, i.e. \(\vert m \vert = l\), and running the same argument:

$$ \begin{aligned} l^2 &\leq l(l+1) \\ l^2 &\leq l^2 + l \\ 0 &\leq l \end{aligned} $$

This is now obviously true for any value of \(l = 0, 1, 2, ...\).

So the largest integer \(m\) that satisfies \(m^2 \leq l(l+1)\) is \(\vert m \vert = l\).

Thus we conclude that the values of \(m\) (the z-component of our quantised angular momentum) that are possible for a given value of \(l\) are:

$$ m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, ... \pm l $$

Which leads directly to the rule that a given angular momentum \(L\) has \(2L+1\) projections. You can think of the it as:

  • Each set of positive and negative integers gives the \(2L\).
  • The zero gives the additional projection, which is the \(+1\)
  • Overall it is \(2L+1\).

A more general way to show this, which also suggests/predicts the existence of half-integer angular momentum, is a bit more involved and uses a lot of angular momentum algebra and ladder operators...
